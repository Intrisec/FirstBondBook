The Mysteries of the Sacred

The hidden biology of the first bond

Before a child can remember anything, the body is already keeping a record. This book follows that record from the first cell to the first smile, through the mother, the father, and the generations behind them.

The question

What happens in the body before the mind can form memories?

A baby’s first real smile, the one aimed at a person, usually arrives between the sixth and the eighth week. By then, nine months and some six weeks of biology have already taken place. An embryo has negotiated with an immune system. A placenta has been built and thrown away. A nervous system has spent months sorting every signal it receives into two kinds, good and bad.

None of this will ever be remembered. A good deal of it stays.

This book describes those mechanisms in plain language, one at a time, in the order they happen. It is not a parenting method and it promises no outcome. It is an account of what is known, who found it, and how firm the finding is.

The mechanism

Three bodies of evidence, one first bond

Each chapter takes a process most readers have never been told about and follows it step by step. These are three of them.

The mother

What a mother feels does not stay inside her.

Her oxytocin barely crosses the placenta, and much of it is cut by an enzyme on the way. The child makes its own. What does arrive is her state: part of her cortisol passes a placental guard that weakens under distress, and a change in her heart and breathing reaches the child within minutes. Her quiet arrives as an absence of alarm.

In the book’s sources: Malek 1996 and Nathan 2021 on placental oxytocin; Gitau 1998 and Monk 2016 on cortisol; DiPietro 2003 on fetal heart rate.

The father

Not a helper. A body that changes.

His contribution begins at the cell: the structure that organizes the embryo’s first division comes from the sperm. After birth his testosterone falls and his oxytocin rises, and the brain regions involved in care respond to how much caring his hands actually do.

In the book’s sources: Sathananthan 1991 on the centriole; Gettler 2011 on testosterone in 624 men; Gordon 2010 on oxytocin in eighty couples; Abraham 2014 on the father’s brain.

The inheritance

Some of what is carried was never lived.

Stress can leave chemical marks on genes that govern the stress response and the oxytocin receptor. In studies of war and famine survivors, parent and child track one another, one generation across. The book follows an illustrative thread through eight generations, says where the evidence stops, and asks what can loosen a lock.

In the book’s sources: Gregory 2009 on OXTR methylation; Yehuda 2016 on Holocaust survivors and their children; Lumey 2007 on the Dutch Hunger Winter; Moerkerke 2024 on marks that moved.

From cell to smile

The order in which it happens

The book is built on a clock. These are the hours it stops at.

  1. Day 1

    The embryo begins sending chemical signals. The mother’s immune system shifts from attack toward tolerance of a body that is half foreign.

  2. Day 6

    The embryo starts building the placenta. A pregnancy test detects the hormone released by that construction work.

  3. Week 12

    A thin layer of cells called the subplate forms deep in the unborn child’s brain. The book calls it the chessboard.

  4. Weeks 15–20

    The oxytocin neurons take their final shape and the stress axis is set at its resting level. The book calls these five weeks the window.

  5. Month 5

    Hearing begins. The mother’s voice arrives through bone and fluid as rhythm and contour, and is recognized at birth.

  6. Months 7–8

    The child’s own store of oxytocin rises many times over. The vagus nerve gains its myelin sheath through the last weeks before birth.

  7. Birth

    Everything constant changes at once. In cord blood, copeptin, the stable marker of vasopressin, stood some three hundred times higher after vaginal birth than after planned cesarean (Fill Malfertheiner 2021).

  8. The first hour

    Left undisturbed, skin to skin, a newborn passes through nine observed stages and reaches the breast on its own, usually within the first hour.

  9. Around day 45

    The first smile aimed at a person. It usually comes between the sixth and the eighth week.

Filled marks are the three windows the book describes.

The chapter most books leave out

A father, learning a language nobody taught him.

Most writing about early life watches the mother. One chapter here follows the father’s biology from a single cell onward. Reading it means reading about the mother and the child as well.

He asks before he gives.In mice, seminal fluid expands the mother’s regulatory immune cells before implantation, preparing tolerance for an embryo that is half his. In humans the evidence is a correlation so far.
He brings the compass.The human egg discards its centriole. The sperm returns it, and the embryo’s first division is organized around it.
Most of his marks are washed away.Within hours of fertilization, the chemical annotations on the paternal genome are actively erased, while the maternal ones are largely kept.
His fires go out.Sperm mitochondria are tagged for destruction. Every person runs on mitochondria inherited through an unbroken line of mothers.
He keeps writing.A woman’s eggs are formed before she is born. Sperm are copied for life, and the number of new mutations a child carries rises with the father’s age.
His body retools for care.In 624 men followed for four and a half years, waking testosterone fell by about a quarter in those who became fathers. In a separate study of eighty couples, fathers’ oxytocin matched the mothers’ and showed up as play.

What the mechanisms suggest

Not blame. What is known.

The book is careful on this point: blame is one more source of stress, and no parent chose what they inherited. Its last chapter turns the biology into things a parent may want to ask for, stage by stage, always together with their own doctor or midwife.

During pregnancy

People in the room. The book’s reading is that oxytocin answers to bodies and not to pictures, and that company matters most in weeks 15 to 20.

At birth

Skin to skin, low light, quiet, and time, wherever that is clinically safe. After a cesarean, contact as soon as it can be had. Clinical necessity comes first, and clinicians judge it.

The first six weeks

Help that arrives in person: someone who cooks, holds the baby, lets the mother sleep. Feeding on demand, or a bottle given with holding and eye contact.

Around day 45

When the first directed smile comes, the suggestion is the simplest in the book. Smile back.

How it is written

The evidence

How firm is the ground?

Some findings in this field come from animal studies, and many are associations, not demonstrated causes. The book’s own Note says which is which, and where the reading is the author’s. The same three levels apply here.

Established in humans

The paternal centriole. Mitochondria passed down the maternal line. New mutations rising with paternal age. Falling testosterone in involved fathers. A placental barrier that stops most, not all, maternal cortisol.

Shown in animals, early in humans

Sperm RNAs that carry traces of a father’s diet or stress. Seminal fluid preparing immune tolerance. Vasopressin sparing newborn neurons, counted in mice and not yet in a human child.

Open questions

Whether inflammation in pregnancy writes on the oxytocin receptor. Which enzyme takes the marks off. How far a mother’s history reaches her child. These connections belong to the author’s framework, ETK, one proposed reading of the literature and not established consensus.
